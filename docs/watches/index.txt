12:T4cc,A 1970s regatta countdown with a Lemania inside. Grey-silver dial with a countdown segment between ten and two — five windows change colour (blue → orange → red → silver) with each phase of the countdown before the start. The yellow centre hand shows the countdown seconds, and the inner scale is divided into minutes 0–60. "RACING" with a crest and "REGATTA AUTOMATIC" on the dial. Unpolished steel tonneau case, crown and two pushers on the right to run the countdown, and a three-masted sailing ship in the waves engraved on the caseback. On its original steel bracelet.

<DidYouKnow logo="/watches/racing-regatta/logo.svg">
Regatta watches with a countdown dial appeared in the early 1960s (Aquastar Régate patent, 1961) as a tool for skippers to time the start of a race precisely — in sailing, unlike most sports, the start is counted down. The mechanism in this piece is related to the Lemania 1345 / Aquastar Régate family (derived from the Felsa 4000N calibre) — through the 1970s and 80s several brands fitted the same type of movement under their own names (Heuer, Aquastar, Wakmann and others), so the same heart turns up today under a surprising number of different names on the dial.
</DidYouKnow>

14:T42d,Seiko 6139 "Pogue" — one of the world's first production automatic chronographs, by many accounts the first on sale (May 1969). The nickname comes from astronaut William Pogue, who wore one on the Skylab 4 mission (1973–74), making it the first automatic chronograph in space. Blue dial with day-date, 30-minute register and a Pepsi tachymeter bezel. The case has a small notch above the crown — a detail of the earliest production in 1969 (before June 1970), later replaced by a smoother shape. Exactly the early detail Pogue collectors look for.

<DidYouKnow logo="/watches/6139-9000/logo.svg">
Calibre 6139 is regarded as the world's first series-produced automatic chronograph — development began in 1968 and it went on sale in Japan in spring 1969, in a tight race with the Zenith El Primero and the Heuer/Breitling/Hamilton group's Chronomatic calibre. The case with the small notch above the crown, which this piece has, was only made during the first year of production and is a sought-after mark of an early piece among collectors today.
</DidYouKnow>

<DidYouKnow logo="/watches/7984/logo.svg">
The Oyster Royal was the entry point to Tudor's Oyster family, which the brand built on cases supplied directly by Rolex. Reference 7984 from the mid-1960s has no date window — a simple three-hand dial with no complications that bet on legibility and reliability rather than features. The same case robustness as the Rolex of the day, at a lower entry price, is why collectors and beginners alike seek these pieces out today.
</DidYouKnow>

Spezimatic was GUB's flagship automatic line of the 1960s and 70s, built on in-house movements developed without Swiss licences. The 26-jewel version is one of the better-equipped variants of the range and today makes an interesting, affordable entry into German — not Swiss — watchmaking history.
</DidYouKnow>
